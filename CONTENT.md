## Rochester: A City Built on Innovation

Rochester, New York has been one of America's most important technology cities for over 200 years. Its story is one of audacious invention, world-changing industries, and the relentless cycle of reinvention that defines great cities.

### The Erie Canal Era (1817–1850s)

The Erie Canal, begun in 1817 and completed in 1825, was America's first great feat of engineering. Built entirely by muscle power, it linked the Great Lakes and the American Midwest to the Hudson River and New York Harbor. The Appalachian Mountains had made overland access to western territory nearly impossible — the canal solved this with elegant design: a system of locks, aqueducts, and ports that allowed barges to traverse 363 miles.

Rochester sat at the canal's crossing of the Genesee River, becoming a boomtown overnight. The city earned the nickname "The Flour City" as grain from the Midwest flowed through its mills. The canal didn't just move goods — it moved ideas, people, and ambition into Rochester.

### The Vision Capital (1880s–1990s)

By the late 19th century, Rochester had transformed into what writer George Friedman called the world capital of "vision management" — the taming, capturing, and perfecting of what humans see. Three companies made Rochester a global technology powerhouse:

#### Eastman Kodak (founded 1888)

George Eastman transformed photography from a cumbersome professional practice into a universal consumer experience. Kodak's genius was taking existing photographic chemistry and engineering it into something anyone could use — "You press the button, we do the rest." The company's name became synonymous with photography itself. At its peak, Kodak employed over 60,000 people in Rochester alone and held 90% of the US film market.

Key innovations born in Rochester:
- Roll film (1885)
- The Brownie camera ($1, 1900) — democratized photography
- Kodachrome color film (1935)
- Digital camera sensor technology (1975 — invented at Kodak, ironically)

#### Xerox (founded as Haloid Company, 1906; became Xerox 1961)

The Xerox 914, the first automatic plain-paper copier, shipped from Rochester in 1959. It transformed how the world handled documents. "Xerox" became a verb — people "xeroxed" copies regardless of the machine's brand. The company's Palo Alto Research Center (PARC) later invented the graphical user interface, the mouse, Ethernet, and laser printing — technologies that shaped the entire personal computer revolution.

#### Bausch & Lomb (founded 1853)

Bausch & Lomb perfected the mass production of optical lenses — first for spectacles, then for microscopes, binoculars, and eventually contact lenses. Their lens technology underpinned both Kodak's cameras and military optics during both World Wars.

### The Connecting Thread

The lens. These weren't three random companies — they were variations on a single technological theme. Lenses that could capture reality (Kodak), reproduce documents (Xerox), or correct human vision (Bausch & Lomb). They shared suppliers, traded employees, and created a dense ecosystem of optical and precision manufacturing expertise. Rochester was to vision technology what Detroit was to automobiles or Silicon Valley would become to microchips.

### The Fall and the Lesson

Through the 1990s, these companies seemed eternal. Then the digital revolution arrived:

- Kodak couldn't accept that chemical film would be surpassed by digital imaging — despite having invented the core sensor technology in-house
- Xerox believed paper documents would always be reproduced on paper
- Bausch & Lomb missed new lens-making technologies

All three eventually recognized the shift, but too late. By the 2010s, Kodak had gone through bankruptcy, Xerox had split and merged, and Bausch & Lomb had been acquired by private equity.

### The New Chapter

Rochester's story isn't a tragedy — it's the setup for what comes next. The city retains world-class universities (University of Rochester, RIT), deep technical talent, affordable cost of living, and the same geographic advantages that made it a boomtown 200 years ago. The question isn't whether Rochester will innovate again — it's what form that innovation takes.

**Roc Startup Week exists to answer that question.**

The founders, students, and builders gathering in November 2026 are writing Rochester's next chapter. They carry the same audacity that built a canal through wilderness, put a camera in every home, and made the copy machine ubiquitous. The difference: this time, the innovation is distributed across hundreds of startups rather than concentrated in three giants.

## Key Messaging

**Tagline options:**
- "Where innovation is nothing new."
- "200 years of audacity. The next chapter starts now."
- "Built by inventors. Rebuilt by founders."

**Core narrative:** Rochester isn't a city trying to become a tech city — it's a tech city remembering what it is. The same conditions that created Kodak, Xerox, and Bausch & Lomb (university talent, precision engineering culture, geographic advantage, and sheer audacity) are producing the next wave of high-growth companies.

**Tone:** Confident, historically grounded, forward-looking. Not nostalgic — purposeful. We honor the past to fuel the future.
